What Is Slemani Kebab? Minced Lamb from the Charcoal Grill

11 August 2026 · 7 min read

Slemani Kabab is the minced-lamb skewer many first-time guests should meet before anything else: shaped by hand, cooked over real charcoal, and served with salad and naan.

On Hawler’s menu the name appears as Slemani Kabab — £14.99 for two shish of minced lamb, grilled over charcoal, with salad and naan. “Slemani” points to Sulaymaniyah (Silêmanî), a major city in the Kurdistan Region known for its own kebab culture. You do not need a thesis in regional food history to enjoy it; you only need to know that this is minced meat treated as a craft, not a shortcut.

What makes a Slemani-style kebab

Unlike cubed tikka, Slemani Kabab starts as seasoned minced lamb pressed along a wide, flat skewer. The cook’s hand leaves ridges that catch flame and hold juices. Over live charcoal those ridges brown, fat renders, and a thin crust forms while the centre stays succulent. The flavour is lamb-forward, spiced enough to be interesting, clean enough that you taste the meat and the smoke rather than a heavy marinade mask.

At Hawler the serving is straightforward: two shish, salad, naan. No towering garnish is required. The drama is on the grill — the same scene captured in close photographs of kofta-style kebabs with flames licking the metal.

How it compares to other kebabs on the menu

Hawler’s grill section is broad. Chicken Kabab uses minced chicken breast. Mahshi Kabab folds parsley and herbs through minced lamb. Iranian Kabab arrives with rice. Lamb tikka and chicken tikka are cubes, not mince. Slemani sits in the classic minced-lamb lane — the plate to order when you want the archetype. If you cannot choose, the Mix 3 Kabab (£20.99) lets you include a Slemani shish beside tikka or chops. Our Iraqi mixed grill guide explains how to build that plate.

Why charcoal matters

A flat-top griddle can brown mince; it cannot perfume it the same way. Charcoal radiates fierce heat and lays down aromatic compounds as fat hits the coals. That is why grill houses keep open fires even in busy London kitchens. For a longer look at the method — skewers, heat management, timing — read the charcoal grilling guide.

How to eat it

Tear the Slemani naan (or house naan), wrap a piece of kebab with salad, and eat with your hands if you like. Lemon on the salad, a spoon of hummus from the complimentary welcome, and you have the full grammar of the meal. Dine-in guests at Hawler receive soup, salad, hummus and Slemani naan on the house — so the kebab you pay for is the centre, not the entire experience.

Who should order it

  • First-time visitors who want the clearest statement of the grill.
  • Lamb lovers who prefer mince to chops.
  • Anyone building a mixed table: one Slemani Kabab plus one Kuzi covers fire and slow cooking.
  • Groups comparing styles — order Slemani beside Mahshi or chicken kebab and taste the difference.

The short answer

Slemani Kabab is minced lamb on the charcoal skewer, served simply with salad and naan — named for a Kurdish grilling tradition and cooked daily on Edgware Road under the Hawler name. Order it when you want the grill without negotiation.

Seasoning, fat and the hand of the cook

Minced kebab lives or dies on the balance of meat to fat and the evenness of the seasoning. Too lean and the skewer dries; too heavy-handed with spice and you lose the lamb. At a proper charcoal house the mince is worked just enough to bind, then pressed onto the skewer in a single confident motion. Those fingerprints and ridges are not rustic decoration — they are cooking geometry, creating more surface for the coals to paint.

Guests sometimes ask whether Slemani Kabab is the same as “kofta” or “kofte” elsewhere. The family resemblance is real: seasoned mince shaped for the grill. The Hawler plate stays specific — two shish, salad, naan, charcoal — rather than hiding under a generic label. Order it by the name on the menu and you will get the minced-lamb experience the kitchen intends.

First visit tips

If you are dining with someone who prefers milder flavours, Slemani is still a friendly entry point: the smoke is present, the chilli usually restrained compared with heavily sauced dishes. Ask for extra lemon or a cooling jajick on the side if you want contrast. Keep phones down when the skewers arrive; minced kebab waits poorly once it leaves the fire, and the first minute of crust is the prize.

Vegetarians at the same table can lean on mezze, falafel and salads while you take the Slemani — hospitality does not require everyone to order identical mains. What matters is that bread and dips stay communal so the meal still feels shared.
