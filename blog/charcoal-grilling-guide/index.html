Charcoal Grilling: How the Mangal Shapes Kurdish and Iraqi Kebab

29 September 2026 · 8 min read

Charcoal grilling is the loud heart of a Kurdish-Iraqi grill house. Understanding the method makes the menu easier to read — and the first bite easier to appreciate.

When people say a kebab tastes “of the fire,” they are naming a process. At Hawler Restaurant, meats such as Slemani Kabab, tikka, chops and mixed shish are cooked over charcoal. A cook stands at a long grill, turning skewers as fat flares and smoke rises — the everyday theatre of a mangal kitchen. This guide explains what that means for flavour, timing and ordering, without pretending you need chef training to enjoy dinner.

Why charcoal instead of a hotplate

A solid steel griddle conducts heat evenly; charcoal radiates it and perfumes it. Dripping fat hits embers and returns as aroma. The exterior of minced kebabs can char in spots while the interior stays moist. Vegetables on the side of a mixed platter — tomato, chilli, onion — blister the same way. That uneven beauty is desirable. Uniform pale brown is not the goal.

Skewers and shapes

Flat metal skewers hold minced kebabs so the meat grips and cooks in a long, even log. Cubed tikka leaves space between pieces for heat to circulate. Chops need careful turning so edges crisp without drying the eye of the meat. Wings want enough time for skin to colour. When you order a mixed grill, you are ordering several of these geometries at once — which is why mixes feel abundant.

What you can taste in Slemani Kabab

Slemani Kabab (£14.99) is the clearest lesson: minced lamb, two shish, salad and naan. The ridges from hand-moulding become crisp edges; the charcoal supplies the rest. Compare it on another visit to chicken kebab or Mahshi Kabab (herbed minced lamb) and you will hear how seasoning and species change the same fire.

Heat, patience and why tables wait a little

Live coals are not a microwave. Busy evenings mean the grill is full; good cooks still refuse to serve grey, under-seared meat. Complimentary soup, salad, hummus and Slemani naan exist partly as hospitality and partly as intelligent pacing — you are eating while the mangal works. Lean into that pause rather than watching the clock.

Pairing fire with the rest of the kitchen

Charcoal is intense. Cool mezze, yoghurt-based jajick, lemony fattoush and soft bread keep the meal balanced. Slow Kuzi offers an alternative rhythm if someone at the table wants zero char. How to order like you respect the grill

  1. Choose a clear hero: Slemani, a Mix 3, or chops.
  2. Add one cooling mezze if you like contrast.
  3. Keep bread in play throughout.
  4. Share across the table so nothing sits and steams itself soft.
  5. Finish with tea once the smoke has done its job.

Charcoal grilling is not nostalgia; it is a contemporary London technique with old roots. Sit near enough to notice the scent when a fresh skewer passes, and you will understand the menu before dessert — or tea — is even offered.

Smoke, seasoning and what “charred” should mean

Desirable charcoal flavour is aromatic, not acrid. Meat should show deep browning and occasional blackened edges where fat flared — not a coat of bitter soot. If you taste only ash, something in the fire management failed; if you taste only spice paste and no smoke, you might as well have used an oven. Hawler’s grill cooking aims at the middle path: readable lamb or chicken, plus the perfume of the mangal.

Marinades on tikka help browning and juiciness; minced kebabs rely more on internal fat and surface seasoning. That is why a mixed grill educates the palate so efficiently — you sample both schools on one platter.

Watching the grill without hovering

Open kitchens and visible grills are part of the trust contract. You do not need to stand over the cook, but noticing the rhythm — skewers rotated, coals tapped, a fresh load laid down — connects appetite to labour. Photograph if you must; then put the phone away so the crust is eaten at its peak. Technique articles are useful; hot kebab is better.

When planning a charcoal-focused evening for guests new to the cuisine, steer them to Slemani Kabab or Mix 3 first, and save complex weekend specials for a second visit. Fire is enough plot for night one.
