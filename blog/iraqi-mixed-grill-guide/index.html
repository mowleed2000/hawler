How to Order an Iraqi Mixed Grill at Hawler

25 August 2026 · 8 min read

A mixed grill is less a single recipe than a way of eating: several skewers, one table, bread within reach. Here is how to order one well at Hawler.

In Iraqi and Kurdish grill houses, the mixed plate is how groups negotiate taste. One person wants minced lamb, another wants chicken tikka, someone else is loyal to chops. Rather than four lonely mains, the kitchen sends a landscape of skewers — often over rice or with salad and naan — and the table sorts itself out. Hawler Restaurant on Edgware Road builds that habit into the menu with clear mix options and an open charcoal grill.

Start with Mix 3 Kabab

The headline mixed option is Mix 3 Kabab at £20.99: three shish of your choice, grilled, served with salad and house naan. It is the right size for a hungry solo diner who wants variety, or a shared centrepiece for two alongside complimentary starters. Popular building blocks include Slemani Kabab (minced lamb), lamb or chicken tikka, chicken kebab, wings and lamb chops (£18 when ordered as their own dish).

Larger mixes exist for bigger tables — including combinations of five or ten shish — so you can scale without inventing a custom banquet from scratch. Check the grill section of the menu for exact compositions and prices.

How to choose your three shish

A useful rule: contrast textures.

  • One minced (Slemani or chicken kebab) for crust and spice.
  • One cubed (lamb or chicken tikka) for marinade and bite-sized pieces.
  • One richer cut (chops or wings) for bone-in flavour.

If the table prefers lamb only, double down on Slemani and lamb tikka, then add chops. If children or milder palates are present, keep one chicken tikka in the mix. Charcoal treats each skewer differently; the point of a mix is to taste that range in one sitting.

What arrives around the meat

Salad and naan are part of the paid grill dishes; dine-in guests also receive complimentary soup, salad, hummus and Slemani naan as the house welcome. That means you can often skip ordering extra bread at the start, then add freshly baked Slemani naan (£1.99) if the table is tearing through loaves. Cold mezze — fattoush, tabbouleh, baba ganoug — refresh the palate between smoky bites; see the starters guide.

Mixed grill versus Kuzi

A mixed grill is a conversation with fire. Kuzi is a conversation with time. Many groups order both: grills for the centre of the table, one Kuzi Lamb Neck (£16) for whoever wants sauce-soaked rice. That combination mirrors how celebratory meals work at home — something from the mangal, something from the pot.

Pacing the meal

Do not rush the complimentary dishes off the table. Use them while the grill works. When skewers arrive, keep lemon, chilli and dips in play. Share assertively; mixed grills punish politeness that leaves one skewer cooling untouched. Tea at the end resets the palate after spice and smoke.

A sample order for two

  1. Settle in; enjoy the complimentary soup, salad, hummus and Slemani naan.
  2. Order one Mix 3 Kabab (Slemani, chicken tikka, lamb chops or your preferred trio).
  3. Add baba ganoug or fattoush if you want extra herbs and smoke-cutting acidity.
  4. Share everything; finish with tea.

That pattern — welcome, fire, share, tea — is the mixed grill as a ritual, not merely a SKU on a delivery app. Order it when the table cannot agree, and let charcoal cast the deciding vote.

Rice nights versus salad nights

Some mixed grills elsewhere arrive on a hill of rice; Hawler’s Mix 3 Kabab is listed with salad and house naan. If you want grain as well, add plain rice, biryani or a Kuzi for whoever prefers the pot. That flexibility is useful: a salad-led grill feels lighter for lunch; rice makes the same skewers feel like a feast after dark.

Grilled tomato, onion and chilli often appear with charcoal plates across the region — sweet, smoky punctuation between bites of meat. Squeeze lemon liberally. Salt the edges of the naan with juices from the salad. Mixed grilling is tactile eating; cutlery is optional more often than menus admit.

Common ordering mistakes

  • Choosing three nearly identical minced shish and wondering why the plate feels monotone — vary the textures.
  • Ignoring the complimentary welcome and stacking paid mezze until the table is crowded before mains arrive.
  • Letting the last skewer sit while everyone finishes conversations; rotate the plate so heat is shared.
  • Forgetting tea at the end, then wondering why spice still lingers on the way home.

Avoid those four and a mixed grill night tends to run itself. The coals do the difficult work; your job is sequencing and sharing.
