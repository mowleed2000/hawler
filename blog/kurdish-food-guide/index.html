A Guide to Kurdish Flavours: What to Order First

14 July 2026 · 8 min read

If you have never sat down to a Kurdish or Iraqi charcoal meal, the menu can feel gloriously crowded. This guide walks you through the flavours that matter most — and what to order first at Hawler on Edgware Road.

Walk into a Kurdish grill house and the first thing that greets you is rarely the bill of fare. It is heat from the coals, the smell of minced lamb catching flame, and — if the house keeps tradition — something already on its way to the table before you have chosen a thing. At Hawler Restaurant, that welcome is deliberate: complimentary soup, salad, hummus and hot Slemani naan for dine-in guests, a small rehearsal of the hospitality the kitchen was named for.

Named after Hawler (Erbil), capital of the Kurdistan Region, the restaurant sits at 355 Edgware Road, London W2 1BS, among one of the capital’s best-known Middle Eastern corridors. Everything is 100% Halal. Opening hours run daily from 12:00 to 23:00. What follows is a practical map of Kurdish and Iraqi flavours as you will meet them here — not a museum tour, but a diner’s route from first bite to last tea.

The flavour map: fire, slow heat, and bread

Kurdish and Iraqi cooking share a wide kitchen with neighbouring cuisines, yet a few signatures keep returning. Charcoal is not a garnish; it is the method. Minced kebabs are pressed onto flat metal skewers and grilled over live coals until the exterior chars and the fat renders. Cubed tikka takes marinade and smoke. Alongside the grill sits a quieter craft: slow braises such as Kuzi, where lamb cooks until it yields over fragrant rice. And through nearly every plate runs warm flatbread — especially Slemani-style naan — meant for tearing, scooping and wrapping.

Think of a meal in three movements: something cool and sharp to open (salad, hummus, herbs), something from the fire or the pot as the centre, and rice or bread to carry sauces home. Sharing is the default. Plates arrive generous enough for conversation to stretch.

What to order first

If it is your first visit, resist the urge to sample everything. Choose one clear path, then return. Three reliable openings:

  • Slemani Kabab (£14.99) — two shish of minced lamb over charcoal, with salad and naan. The purest introduction to Hawler’s grill.
  • Kuzi Lamb Neck (£16) — slow-cooked lamb with rice and a sauce. Softer, deeper, ideal if you prefer braise to sear.
  • Mix 3 Kabab (£20.99) — three shish of your choice. Best when two people want variety without overcrowding the table.

Lamb chops (£18) suit diners who want bone-in richness from the same coals. For a fuller picture of how plates are built, browse the full menu before you arrive, then let the complimentary starters settle you in.

Starters that sharpen the appetite

Beyond the welcome dishes, cold mezze such as fattoush, tabbouleh, baba ganoug and jajick (cucumber yoghurt) cut through smoke and fat. A Mix Platter gathers several dips if the table wants to graze. Hot bites — kibbeh in potato, bulghur or rice shells, falafel — add crunch before the skewers land. Read more in our Middle Eastern starters guide.

Rice, sauces and the slow side of the kitchen

Kuzi is the house’s celebrated slow dish: lamb neck or shank over rice, with a sauce you choose. Okra, white beans, potato, aubergine or a spicier tabsee-style sauce each change the plate’s mood. Biryani appears too, for guests who want spice layered through the grain rather than poured beside it. If charcoal is the loud voice of the kitchen, these pots are the steady one — see what Kuzi is for a closer look.

How a typical table unfolds

Arrive hungry but not frantic. Let the complimentary soup, salad, hummus and Slemani naan arrive. Order a grill for the table or a Kuzi each if preferences diverge. Add one cold mezze if you want herbs and lemon in every other bite. Leave room for tea; Kurdish hospitality treats the close of the meal as part of the hosting, not an afterthought.

Portions are built for sharing. Two people often do well with one Mix 3 Kabab plus a cold starter, or Slemani Kabab alongside a bowl of hummus. Larger groups can scale with multi-shish mixes from the grill section of the menu.

Halal cooking, London timing

Every dish is 100% Halal. That matters for many guests on Edgware Road and for anyone planning a family lunch or late dinner after work. Hawler is open daily 12:00–23:00 — early enough for a leisurely midday meal, late enough for a post-theatre or post-shift grill. For directions and practical visit details, see Visit.

The complimentary welcome is a dine-in tradition. If you are staying home, the same charcoal and slow dishes travel via Uber Eats, Deliveroo and Just Eat — start from Order. For celebrations or office lunches, ask about options on the catering page; availability depends on what the kitchen can prepare for your date.

A short glossary for the menu

  • Slemani Kabab — minced lamb kebab associated with Sulaymaniyah (Slemani) style grilling.
  • Kuzi / Qozi — slow lamb (or chicken) with rice and sauce.
  • Tikka — marinated cubes of meat on the skewer.
  • Naan / Slemani naan — house flatbread, blistered and soft, for tearing.
  • Baba ganoug — smoky aubergine dip with tahini and yoghurt notes.
  • Jajick — cooling cucumber yoghurt.

Once those words feel familiar, the menu stops looking long and starts looking like choices. Begin with fire or begin with the pot; either path is honest to the region the restaurant is named for.
