Hawler and Erbil: The Name Behind the Restaurant

1 September 2026 · 7 min read

The restaurant is called Hawler because Hawler is Erbil — one of the world’s oldest continuously inhabited cities, and the capital of the Kurdistan Region. The name is a compass, not a slogan.

On a London menu, “Hawler” can look like a brand invented for pronounceability. It is not. Hawler (also rendered Hawlêr) is the Kurdish name for Erbil, the capital of the Kurdistan Region of Iraq. The restaurant at 355 Edgware Road takes that name as its frame: Kurdish and Iraqi charcoal cooking, Halal by default, hospitality first.

The citadel as a landmark of memory

Erbil’s citadel rises on an ancient tell above the modern city. At sunset the stone catches amber light while the park and fountain below fill with evening walkers — a scene of ordinary civic life against extraordinary depth of time. You do not need to have visited to understand why a London grill house would borrow the name: it signals origin, continuity and pride without requiring a history lecture at the table.

What travels to Edgware Road is not a replica of a single Erbil restaurant. It is a set of habits — charcoal grilling, slow rice dishes such as Kuzi, warm Slemani naan, tea, and the assumption that guests are hosted rather than merely served. Read how that hosting feels in practice in our essay on Kurdish hospitality.

Kurdish and Iraqi on one menu

Food cultures in the region overlap. Minced kebabs, aubergine dips, stuffed vine leaves, biryani-style rice and yoghurt drinks appear in neighbouring kitchens with local accents. Hawler’s menu reads as Kurdish and Iraqi together: Slemani Kabab nodding to Sulaymaniyah’s grilling fame, Kuzi and Qozi spelling the slow lamb tradition, pacha and dolma available on weekend days as listed on the menu. The point is recognition for diners who grew up with these plates, and a clear invitation for those who did not.

Why the name matters on Edgware Road

Edgware Road has long been a destination for Middle Eastern food in London. Amid that density, a precise name helps. “Hawler” tells Kurdish guests they may find flavours of home; it tells everyone else that the kitchen has a geographic heart, not a generic “grill” label. For a wider look at eating this cuisine in the neighbourhood, see Kurdish and Iraqi food on Edgware Road.

What the kitchen actually serves under that name

Heritage claims are empty without plates. Hawler’s everyday evidence is concrete: 100% Halal meat, charcoal kebabs including Slemani Kabab (£14.99) and Mix 3 Kabab (£20.99), Kuzi Lamb Neck (£16), lamb chops (£18), complimentary soup, salad, hummus and Slemani naan for dine-in, and hours daily from 12:00 to 23:00. Phone 020 7724 5757; address London W2 1BS. Delivery runs through Uber Eats, Deliveroo and Just Eat via Order.

Visiting without inventing a myth

This article will not invent an owner biography, a founding year wrapped in legend, or awards the restaurant has not asked us to claim. The honest story is simpler: a grill house named for Erbil’s Kurdish name, cooking dishes that belong to that wider culinary world, located where Londoners already go to seek them. If you want the flavours before the geography lesson, start with the Kurdish food guide. If you want the table’s social rules, start with hospitality. If you want the city outside the door, start with the Edgware Road piece.

Language on the fascia, flavours on the plate

Using the Kurdish name Hawler in a London streetscape is a small act of cultural clarity. English-language menus still need words like kebab, hummus and tikka that travellers already recognise; the restaurant name carries the part that is easier to erase. Together they say: you can order with familiar vocabulary, and you are still in a house that points toward Erbil.

Diners sometimes confuse Kurdish cuisine with a single national stereotype. In reality, mountain and city kitchens differ, family recipes travel with diaspora communities, and restaurant menus adapt to local supply. Hawler’s Edgware Road list — charcoal mixes, Kuzi, Slemani naan, weekend specials when available — is one living snapshot of that broader world, not a museum catalogue of every village dish.

Eating as remembrance without solemnity

Heritage food can tip into earnestness. The better nights are joyful: children tearing naan, elders arguing politely over which sauce belongs with lamb neck, friends comparing tikka to mince. Naming the restaurant after Erbil does not require a history seminar between courses. If someone at the table is curious, point them to the citadel photograph, the complimentary welcome, and the Halal charcoal grill — sensory facts first, encyclopedia later.

When you leave, the street returns to London neon and bus noise. The point of the name is that for an hour or two the table faced another capital.
